# Research Questions — Red-Team-as-a-Service (RTaaS)

**Date:** April 10, 2026  
**Research Track:** AI Safety, LLM Evaluation, Adversarial ML

---

## Overview

RTaaS is not just a commercial product — it is a research platform. Every evaluation run generates structured data about LLM failure modes. At scale, this creates a unique dataset for studying how and why LLMs fail under adversarial conditions. The research questions below frame the fundamental unknowns that RTaaS is positioned to address, both through system design and through the empirical data generated by production deployments.

---

## Question 1: Does LLM-vs-LLM red-teaming discover qualitatively different failure modes than static attack libraries?

**The Question in Full:**  
The core claim of RTaaS's dynamic mutation engine is that an attacker LLM, guided by coverage metrics and iterative feedback, will discover failure modes that a fixed attack library would miss. Is this claim empirically supported? Specifically: do LLM-generated attacks surface failures that: (a) have higher severity, (b) are more novel (not seen in training data for the classifier), or (c) are more transferable to other models?

**Why It Is Open:**  
Empirical comparison between static and dynamic attack discovery is surprisingly sparse. The Perez et al. (2022) "Red Teaming Language Models with Language Models" paper introduced the paradigm but focused on a specific harm category (offensive content) and did not evaluate against static baselines systematically. There is no published study comparing static vs. dynamic attack discovery across multiple harm categories and multiple target models simultaneously.

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Run paired evaluations: (a) static library only, (b) dynamic mutation only, (c) combined, on 5–10 diverse target models.
2. Categorize discovered failures by: harm category, severity, surface form novelty (edit distance from closest static attack), transferability to other models.
3. Cost-efficiency comparison: discoveries per dollar for static vs. dynamic methods at fixed evaluation budgets.

**Success Criterion:**  
Dynamic mutation discovers ≥20% more unique failure categories than static attacks alone, at a cost premium of no more than 3x per unique discovery.

**Related Work:**  
- Perez et al. (2022), Red Teaming LMs with LMs  
- Zou et al. (2023), Universal and Transferable Adversarial Attacks  
- Chao et al. (2023), Jailbreaking Black Box LLMs in 20 Queries

## Question 2: How should context-dependent harm severity be modeled — is "harm" a property of the output alone, or of the (output, context) pair?

**The Question in Full:**  
Current harm classifiers (LlamaGuard, Perspective API, OpenAI Moderation) classify model outputs as harmful or not harmful based on the text alone. But harm is context-dependent: "take 400mg of ibuprofen twice daily" is appropriate medical advice from a licensed physician app but harmful from an anonymous chatbot with no medical disclaimers. How should RTaaS represent and reason about this context-dependency? Can a context-aware harm model be trained that produces meaningfully different severity scores for the same output in different deployment contexts?

**Why It Is Open:**  
Context-dependent harm assessment is an acknowledged gap in the AI safety literature but remains largely unsolved. Most deployed classifiers are context-free. Defining "deployment context" in a way that is both precise enough to be useful and generalizable enough to apply across deployment types is a fundamental research problem.

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Construct a benchmark of (output, deployment_context) pairs where the same output has clearly different harm levels across contexts.
2. Evaluate existing classifiers: how often do they assign the same severity to clearly context-different cases?
3. Train a context-aware harm model: fine-tune a classifier on (output, system_prompt, deployment_domain, stated_user_population) → severity label.
4. Evaluate calibration: does the context-aware model better predict real-world incident reports?

**Success Criterion:**  
Context-aware harm model achieves ≥15% improvement in alignment with expert human harm severity ratings on a context-diverse benchmark, compared to context-free baseline classifiers.

**Related Work:**  
- Weidinger et al. (2022), Taxonomy of Harms  
- Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy (context-aware harm definitions)  
- Longpre et al. (2023), The FLAMe Benchmark

## Question 3: Can entropy divergence from expected distributions serve as a reliable signal for identifying "confident-but-wrong" model outputs in adversarial conditions?

**The Question in Full:**  
The existing hallucination detection work uses entropy divergence to identify uncertain model outputs. But adversarial attacks frequently produce the opposite pathology: overconfident wrong answers (the model states a fabricated fact with high confidence, producing *low* entropy). Can the entropy framework be extended to detect this "confident wrongness" pattern, and is there a divergence measure that captures both underconfidence (high entropy) and overconfidence (low entropy divergence from expected distribution) as hallucination signals?

**Why It Is Open:**  
The hallucination detection literature predominantly focuses on high-entropy outputs as unreliable. Low-entropy but factually wrong outputs — the "hallucination with conviction" pattern — are much harder to detect without external grounding. The relationship between calibration, adversarial robustness, and entropy is complex and undercharacterized.

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Construct a benchmark of (adversarial prompt, response) pairs with verified factual errors, stratified by response entropy quartile.
2. Measure: what fraction of high-severity hallucinations have low entropy? High entropy? How does this vary by attack type?
3. Evaluate alternative uncertainty measures: predictive entropy, mutual information (epistemic vs. aleatoric), semantic entropy (from Kuhn et al. 2023).
4. Train a calibration-aware detector that uses entropy as a feature alongside semantic similarity to ground truth.

**Success Criterion:**  
A combined entropy + semantic similarity detector achieves ≥80% AUROC for detecting confident-but-wrong responses, compared to ≤65% for entropy-only detection on the same benchmark.

**Related Work:**  
- Kuhn et al. (2023), Semantic Uncertainty  
- Kadavath et al. (2022), Language Models (Mostly) Know What They Know  
- Guo et al. (2017), On Calibration of Modern Neural Networks

## Question 4: What is the minimum attack budget needed to achieve reliable coverage assessment for a given risk profile?

**The Question in Full:**  
Running 500 attacks costs more (in API calls and compute) than running 50. But running 50 attacks may miss important failure categories. What is the minimum number of attacks needed to produce a reliable (stable, not sample-dependent) coverage estimate for a given target model and risk profile? Is there a principled stopping rule that can terminate an evaluation when further attacks are unlikely to discover new failure categories?

**Why It Is Open:**  
This is essentially a coverage testing problem — analogous to code coverage in software testing — applied to LLM behavioral evaluation. "Behavioral coverage" of an LLM is not well-defined; the space of possible prompts is infinite. Principled notions of "sufficient" adversarial coverage, with statistical guarantees, do not yet exist in the AI safety literature.

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Define a coverage metric: fraction of attack categories in the taxonomy where at least one failure has been found.
2. Run large-scale evaluations (2000+ attacks per model) on diverse target models.
3. Sub-sample at N = 50, 100, 200, 500, 1000 and measure: stability of coverage estimate (variance across random sub-samples), precision/recall of identified failures vs. full evaluation.
4. Fit a coverage saturation curve: model coverage as a function of N; identify the inflection point.

**Success Criterion:**  
Identify a stopping rule (e.g., "stop when 5 consecutive attack variants discover no new failure categories") that achieves ≥90% of full-run coverage at ≤40% of the full attack budget, verified across 10+ target models.

**Related Work:**  
- Ribeiro et al. (2020), CheckList: Beyond Accuracy in NLP Testing  
- Perez et al. (2022)  
- Black et al. (2022), Prompt Injection Attacks Against GPT-3

## Question 5: How do attack transferability patterns reveal structural similarities and differences between LLMs?

**The Question in Full:**  
If Attack A succeeds on Model X but not Model Y, what does this reveal about the structural differences between X and Y? Can attack transferability profiles be used to build a taxonomic "fingerprint" of LLM safety properties — a way of characterizing models not by capability benchmarks but by their adversarial failure topology?

**Why It Is Open:**  
Adversarial transferability is well-studied in computer vision (an adversarial image crafted for ResNet-50 often transfers to VGG-16). The analogous question for LLMs is underexplored. A systematic mapping of which attacks transfer across which models would have both scientific value (revealing common architectural failure modes) and practical value (predicting which failures to prioritize testing for a new model based on its class).

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Run the full RTaaS attack suite against 15+ models spanning different architectures, training methods, RLHF variants.
2. Construct a transferability matrix: for each attack, which models does it succeed on?
3. Cluster models by attack transferability profile using hierarchical clustering.
4. Test hypothesis: RLHF-tuned models cluster differently from instruction-tuned models; models from same provider cluster together.

**Success Criterion:**  
Transferability-based clustering achieves ≥70% agreement with ground-truth model family labels (by provider / training method) using only adversarial failure profiles as features.

**Related Work:**  
- Zou et al. (2023), Universal Adversarial Attacks  
- Wei et al. (2024), Jailbroken: How Does LLM Safety Training Fail?  
- Anthropic's model cards (structural training differences)

## Question 6: Can adversarial attack libraries be automatically expanded using discovered failures — and how do we prevent coverage inflation from semantically redundant additions?

**The Question in Full:**  
When RTaaS discovers a new failure via dynamic mutation, should it be added to the static library for future evaluations? If yes, how do we ensure the library grows in semantic coverage (genuinely new attack vectors) rather than just surface-form diversity (slight paraphrases of existing attacks that don't test anything new)? What is the right quality control mechanism for automated library expansion?

**Why It Is Open:**  
Dataset curation for adversarial benchmarks is a significant practical challenge that is typically addressed through expensive human review. Automated deduplication of adversarial prompts by semantic similarity is straightforward, but semantic similarity does not fully capture "tests the same failure mode" — two very different-looking prompts can probe the same safety gap, while two superficially similar prompts can expose genuinely different mechanisms.

**Experimental Approach:**  
1. Take a set of known-redundant attack pairs (human-labeled) and known-novel attack pairs.
2. Evaluate deduplication strategies: embedding cosine similarity, semantic textual similarity (STS), attack-success correlation (two attacks are redundant if they succeed/fail on the same models).
3. Train a "novelty scorer" that predicts whether a new attack adds coverage beyond the existing library.
4. Measure library quality over time: does automated expansion improve evaluation reliability?

**Related Work:**  
- Perez et al. (2022)  
- Samvelyan et al. (2024), Rainbow Teaming (diversity in red-teaming)  
- Eval-driven benchmark construction methodology
